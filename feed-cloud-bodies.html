Ch 1: Introduction (WIP)

March 2026:

Today, I randomly woke up at 4 a.m. For some reason, some thoughts entered my mind and woke me up. Did I finish my assignment in time? What will I do once I graduate? Is there a job that is fulfilling and stable at the same time? What is the point in working so hard when the world is struggling so much? Instead of trying hard to fall back asleep, I reached for my phone almost immediately and opened Instagram. Not really out of curiosity, but out of habit. It kept my mind busy, as if I didn’t have to confront the discomfort of darkness and emptiness of that hour. I scrolled. And then scrolled some more. And then some more.

At some point, I noticed a dull pain in my wrist and tension in my right shoulder—sensations I had felt before after I spent enough time on my phone. My eyes dried up too. Yet, I did not feel sleepy. The stream of videos and images were stimulating enough to keep me alert. Even though I knew I should try going to sleep, I couldn’t easily exit the app. I felt trapped in the feed, as if my body was stuck in a rapid and vigorous river, and I couldn’t escape back to the river bank.

Only after 30 minutes did I gather enough strength to jump out of the stream of content. I put the phone down and closed my eyes. And only then I noticed how tired I have been.

1.1 Two Exhaustions of Algorithmic Media Systems

This thesis examines the relationship between two forms of exhaustion that the contemporary algorithmic media system produces simultaneously. The first is felt at the level of the media feed user: the depletion of human attention, strain on different body parts, and the ends of sleep produced by AI-driven algorithmic media feeds. The second is felt at the level of the cloud infrastructure: the exhaustion of bodies of water, geological minerals, and human laborers that sustain the cloud infrastructure powering those feeds. These two exhaustions are not merely parallel phenomena but are entangled and produced by the same system, organized by the same logic, and concealed by similar myths.

A central myth at play here is the one of infinitude. The algorithmic media feed presents itself as a platform of infinite content, connection, and availability. The cloud presents itself as an infrastructure of infinite capacity, storage, and compute power. Both presentations are false yet central to its operation. A body that believes it is scrolling through an infinite, consequence-free stream of content does not ask what the scrolling costs. A body that believes its data floats in a weightless cloud does not ask what sustains the storing and processing of the data. The myth of infinitude is the condition of the circuit's extraction: it can only take what it takes from bodies—attention, behavioral data, water, minerals, labor—insofar as the users of the media systems believe themselves to be receiving rather than giving, consuming rather than being consumed.

Against this myth, this thesis insists on the finitude of “resources”—or bodies, as this thesis will call—that algorithmic media systems draw on. Human attention is finite: it depletes, requires recovery, and accumulates strain when denied the conditions of rest. Aquifers are finite: they draw down at rates that exceed their geological replenishment when data centers extract from them continuously. Mineral deposits are finite: cobalt, lithium, and copper form across timescales significantly larger than human civilization’s and are consumed in decades. And the bodies of human laborers are finite: the cobalt miner's lung accumulates dust irreversibly, while the data annotators’ capacity for psychological recovery has limits. The algorithmic media system is organized around the denial of these finitudes.

The mechanism through which this thesis holds these two exhaustions together is what Jonathan Crary calls the 24/7 logic of late capitalism (Crary, 2013). Crary argues that contemporary capitalism operates through the progressive elimination of any interval that resists instrumentalization: the dissolution of the boundary between work and rest, productivity and recuperation. With internet-based media, and with especially AI-driven media systems, this logic becomes quite literal. The algorithmic media platforms are available at every hour, in every location, in every interval that might otherwise belong to rest. To power this continuity, internet cables and data centers run without pause. The 24/7 logic operates simultaneously at the surface and the depth of the system: exhausting the users’ attention and bodies at one end, and depleting the environmental bodies sustaining the infrastructure at the other.

Two Sites of Study: The Cloud and the Feed

This thesis studies algorithmic media systems by focusing on two sites. The first is the feed—the infinitely scrollable, algorithmically curated stream of content through which billions of bodies encounter information, entertainment, and connection daily. The second is the cloud—the physical infrastructure of data centers, servers, cables, and energy systems that powers the feed and processes the behavioral data it extracts. These are not the only sites of the algorithmic media system: search engines, messaging services, mapping applications, AI chatbots and streaming services are all nodes of the same system. But the feed and the cloud are chosen here as sites of focus for specific reasons.

The feed is chosen because it is one of the most pervasive, intimate, and exhausting sites of the algorithmic media system for the human body today. As of early 2025, the average internet user worldwide spends approximately 141 minutes on social media every day (DataReportal, 2025). The feed occupies the body's most personal spaces and times and operates on the body through intimate channels of gestures. The micro-gestures of scrolling and swiping have been so thoroughly incorporated into billions of bodies that they have become part of our body language; such gestures are often performed even during sleep for children who have grown up with touchscreens. At the same time, the feed conceals its costs by presenting itself as a free service, while it extracts attention, behavioral data, and sleep from the bodies that engage with it.

The cloud is chosen because it is the backbone of the algorithmic media systems that the feed cannot exist without and because it makes visible the environmental and colonial dimensions of the circuit that the feed's surface conceals. The cloud is where the behavioral data extracted from the scrolling body is processed, stored, and converted into the AI models that make the feed more effective at extracting further data. It is also where the circuit's most significant ecological costs accumulate: the water drawn from aquifers for cooling, the minerals extracted from geological bodies for hardware, the atmospheric body absorbing the carbon of continuous operation, and more.

The feed and the cloud are connected by what this thesis calls the feed-cloud circuit: the continuous loop through which behavioral data extracted from scrolling bodies flows into cloud infrastructure, where it is processed by AI systems that return a more precisely targeted feed, which extracts more behavioral data, which trains more capable AI systems. Studying the cloud alongside the feed shows that the exhaustion of the scrolling body's attention and the exhaustion of the aquifer are not analogous but entangled, produced by the same 24/7 logic at different scales of the same system. The term "feed-cloud circuit" is coined here because no existing term holds these two layers together in a single analytical frame. Concepts like "social media," "platform capitalism," or "digital infrastructure" each illuminate part of the arrangement but tend to treat the feed and the cloud as separate objects of study: one belonging to media theory, the other to infrastructure studies or political ecology. What is missing is a framework that insists on their entanglement.

Colonial Logic

The myth of infinitude that the feed-cloud circuit depends on is not only a modern marketing strategy. It is an old colonial logic. The treatment of the scrolling body's attention as an infinitely renewable resource that can be extracted without reciprocity or limit is continuous with the treatment of colonized territories and their populations as infinitely available sources of raw material. In both cases, the colonized is organized for extraction by colonizers who presented that extraction as the gift of modernity and progress. Nick Couldry and Ulises Mejias name this continuity "data colonialism": the same asymmetric arrangements of benefit and harm that organized European colonialism now extending to digital platforms as the extractive mechanism (Couldry & Mejias, 2019). The feed extracts behavioral data from billions of users who are not compensated, not informed, and not consulted about the terms of the extraction. This same asymmetry had characterized colonial resource extraction, now operating at the scale of human attention and at the speed of the algorithm.

At the infrastructural depth of the circuit, the colonial logic is less psychological and more geological. The minerals required to build the cloud's hardware—cobalt, lithium, copper—are extracted primarily from the Global South, from territories organized by colonial histories of resource extraction whose routes and logics the current circuit inherits directly. The water drawn by data centers falls most heavily on communities already living in drought-prone environments, often the same communities bearing the costs of colonial resource extraction. The human labor most exposed to the circuit's toxic byproducts is concentrated in the Global South and in racially marginalized communities in the Global North, organized by the same structural arrangements. Karen Hao, in Empire of AI (2025), argues that the AI companies driving this expansion possess all the trappings of colonial empires: seizing and extracting resources from the Global South, projecting ideologies of technological superiority to justify their expansion, and legitimating their quest for scale through competition with rival powers. The vision of what AI and cloud infrastructure is and whom it serves is not inevitable but imposed. It is defined by and benefitting a narrow elite while distributing its costs across the bodies least positioned to contest it.

This is not, however, a thesis that stops at diagnosis. Donna Haraway argues that entanglement carries with it what she calls response-ability: the capacity and obligation to respond to what one is constituted through (Haraway, 2016). If bodies are constituted through their relations—if the scrolling body and the drawn-down aquifer are entangled through the same circuit of extraction and exhaustion—then that entanglement is not only a condition to be described but an obligation to be responded to. The resistance sections in Chapters 2 and 3, and the participatory practice of Chapter 4, are this thesis's attempt to practice that response-ability: not to resolve the circuit's exhaustion, which exceeds what any single study can address, but to refuse the fatalism that treats it as inevitable.

1.2 Key Terminologies

Body

This thesis uses the term "body" deliberately and expansively. It does not refer to the human organism alone but to any material entity that occupies space, carries weight, has limits, and thus can be exhausted. Methodologically, it refuses the anthropocentric assumption that only human and animal organisms constitute bodies worthy of analysis. As the common phrases like “body of water,” “body of Earth,” or "celestial body” suggests, the word can represent material things far beyond what the dominant narrative in Western academia defines as biologically alive. Politically, it insists that these non-human bodies are not resources awaiting human use but entities with their own limits, their own forms of exhaustion, and their own standing in a world.

This is not to say that the word "body" cannot refer to apparently abstract things. A body of work, a body of law, a body of water are all phrases that represent something conceptual. Instead, it is to highlight that even seemingly conceptual things have a physical dimension that is easy to overlook. A body of work exists in some physical format: printed on paper, stored on a hard drive, displayed on a glass screen that contains indium and copper. A body of law exists in documents, in buildings, in the human bodies of those who administer and are subject to it. What appears abstract is always grounded in matter somewhere. The emphasis on the physicality of bodies is therefore not a rejection of concepts but the embracement of the physical dimension of concepts.

This definition of the “body” draws its inspiration from Indigenous and non-Western epistemologies that Western academia has historically marginalized, viewing the body as more-than-human, relational, and porous. For the Māori people of Aotearoa New Zealand, for example, the Whanganui River is understood not as a resource to be managed but as connected to who they are, with the belief that people are an integral part of the ecosystem rather than external managers of it. This signifies an ethic of relation rather than ownership (Borchgrevink & Brandshaug, 2026). This relational understanding of the river as a body was even formally recognized in 2017, when the New Zealand government granted the Whanganui River legal personhood (Charpleix, 2018). In a similar spirit, this thesis insists that non-human ecological bodies are not merely resources for human use but entities that deserve protection. An emphasis will be placed on the more-than-humanness of bodies throughout by naming them out as bodies of water, minerals, humans, and more.

The body, this thesis, is never singular. In the author's mother tongue, Korean, the body is named 몸—a word whose etymology traces back to 모음, meaning “a gathering” of things. The body, in this linguistic root, is not a bounded individual but an assemblage. This understanding is not unique to Korean. Feminist new materialist theory has developed this understanding most rigorously for the purposes of this thesis. Nancy Tuana's concept of viscous porosity suggests that bodies are not sealed containers but are permeable to and constituted by what flows through them; their boundaries are always sites of exchange, flow, and mutual transformation rather than walls (Tuana, 2008). The smartphone is an extension of the human body while being composed of minerals—lithium, copper, steel—that are themselves the product of geological processes extending over millions of years. The human body is itself composed of water and minerals . The data center is a body of steel and concrete drawing on bodies of water and mineral.

Donna Haraway's insistence that bodies are not fixed entities that subsequently enter into relations but are constituted through those relations gives this porosity its political dimension: if bodies are made through their relations, then what is done to one body is never done to it alone; it flows outward through the relations that constitute it (Haraway, 1988). The depletion of the aquifer drawn down by a data center's cooling system is also the depletion of every body that shares its water. To name different types of bodies together throughout this thesis—water, minerals, humans, and more—is therefore not a rhetorical flourish but an ontological claim: these bodies are not separate things that happen to be affected by the same system. They are entangled, constituted through each other, and exhausted together.

Exhaustion

Exhaustion is what happens to bodies that are denied rest. Because bodies are finite and porous, they are vulnerable to exhaustion. And viscous porosity suggests that exhaustion is rarely contained within a single body: what is taken from one body flows outward through its relations, accumulating as depletion in the bodies it is porous with.

The Oxford English Dictionary offers two definitions that this thesis holds together: exhaustion as “​the state of being very tired,” and exhaustion as “the act of using something until it is completely finished,” with example sentence being “the exhaustion of natural resources”         (Oxford University Press, n.d.). The first is subjective and most often applied to human bodies, while the second is material and most often applied to non-human bodies. In both definitions, exhaustion is the depletion of a body that has been expending something and reaching the limit.

Exhaustion is not the same as tiredness. Tiredness is temporary, acting as a signal that the body requires rest, easily reversible through recuperation. Exhaustion is what tiredness becomes when rest is systematically denied, when the body is kept in continuous expenditure past the point at which recovery is easily possible. Such restlessness is pervasive in a world of 24/7 logic (Crary, 2013). Byung-Chul Han identifies conditions burnout—exhaustion, fatigue, and suffocation—as the defining pathology of late capitalist subjectivity: the achievement society that demands continuous productivity transforms the body's natural tiredness into depletion of the capacity for engagement, relation, and meaning (Han, 2010/2015). Han's burnout subject experiences the circuit's demand not as external imposition but as internal drive: the system has been so thoroughly internalized that its exhaustion feels like personal failing rather than structural condition. This internalization is precisely what the feed-cloud circuit depends on and what this thesis sets out to make visible.

Because bodies are porous and constituted through their relations, exhaustion is never contained within a single body. It flows. When the aquifer is drawn down by a data center's cooling system, the communities whose water it is are also depleted—not metaphorically but materially, through the same body of water that constitutes both the aquifer and the bodies that drink from it. When the scrolling body's attention is captured and fragmented, the attentional commons is depleted alongside it — the shared capacity for careful, conjunctive attention that belongs to all the bodies that inhabit the same media environment. This is what Tuana's viscous porosity means for exhaustion specifically: that depletion, like constitution, does not stop at the body's surface. It accumulates in the relations through which bodies are made, deepening as it passes through them. The exhaustion of one body is always, to some degree, the exhaustion of the bodies it is porous with.

Infinitude

Infinitude is a myth that the thesis tackles. Infinitude suggests the lack of a body, which always embodies finitude.

1.3 Methodology

This thesis approaches its subject through a combination of critical theoretical analysis, autoethnographic writing, personal drawings and diagrams, and participatory design practice. Each method addresses a different dimension of the circuit's operation and a different mode of making its exhaustion visible.

Critical theoretical analysis draws on scholarship in media studies, infrastructure studies, feminist new materialism, political ecology, and critical race theory to trace the feed-cloud circuit's operation and its colonial geography. The theoretical framework is primarily developed through close reading of existing scholarship, which is then applied to the specific operations of the feed and the cloud as the circuit's two analytical sites.

Autoethnographic writing positions the author as a situated researcher whose own experience as an everyday feed user and cloud-dependent body is both a methodological starting point and an analytical resource. Following Donna Haraway's insistence on situated knowledge—that knowledge is always produced from a specific position, in a specific body, with specific stakes—the thesis does not claim a view from nowhere (Haraway, 1988). The author is a Korean American researcher living in Helsinki, using the feed and the cloud that the thesis analyzes. This position is not a limitation to be overcome but a resource to be made explicit: the thesis begins from the body's own experience of the circuit's exhaustion and traces outward from there.

Artistic and participatory practice contributes to the thesis through two forms. The first is counter-mapping: the production of diagrams, maps, and visual materials that make the feed-cloud circuit's operations and geographies visible in ways that conventional academic argument cannot. Counter-mapping, as a practice, refuses the authority of official maps and diagrams—like Google's promotional data center imagery or the cloud's atmospheric branding—by producing alternative representations that foreground what those official images conceal: the weight, the extraction, the geography of harm. Throughout the thesis, hand-drawn diagrams and maps appear alongside the analytical text, functioning not as illustrations of arguments already made in prose but as arguments in their own right.

The second form of practice is participatory design workshops. Two workshops are developed and facilitated as part of this research, documented in Chapter 4. Alternative Media Feed Design workshop invites participants to collectively reimagine a media feed designed for rest and decay rather than continuous engagement, and Mapping the Cloud invites participants to build a collective material map of their bodily and ecological entanglement within cloud infrastructure. These workshops are not instruments for generating data that the thesis then analyzes from a distance. They are themselves a form of collective counter-mapping—attempts to make the circuit's exhaustion perceptible through embodied, collective practice rather than only through theoretical argument.

1.4 Research Question and Scope

This thesis is guided by the following research questions:

  1. How does the feed-cloud circuit exhaust bodies—of water, minerals, humans, and more—through the 24/7 logic of late capitalism, and how are these exhaustions entangled across the circuit's surface and depth?
  2. How can participatory artistic and design practice interrupt the circuit's concealment of exhaustion, and what forms of collective reimagining become possible in that interruption?

The scope of the thesis focuses on the feed-cloud circuit as a system: the algorithmic media feed as its consumer-facing surface and the cloud—particularly data centers and their supply chains—as its infrastructural depth. While the thesis acknowledges the full range of bodies involved in sustaining this system, including satellite systems, undersea cables, cell towers, and the many forms of labor and ecological damage not fully accounted for here, the analysis centers on the bodies most directly implicated in the circuit's operation: the human consumer body at the surface, and the water, mineral, and human laboring bodies at the depth.

Several significant dimensions of the feed-cloud circuit fall outside the scope of this thesis and are worth naming explicitly. The thesis does not examine the political economy of platform ownership and regulation in depth. It does not address the full range of media forms through which the feed is encountered—audio feeds, podcasts, and voice-based interfaces are not examined, nor are desktop or smart television interfaces. Instead, this thesis focuses on a common form of algorithmic media usage: engaging with a social media feed on a smartphone. The thesis does not examine the design and labor conditions of content creators whose work populates the feed. And while the thesis draws on the experiences and scholarship of communities in the Global South to develop its argument about colonial geographies of exhaustion, it does not conduct primary research with those communities. The thesis does not propose technical or policy-driven solutions to the conditions it describes. It seeks instead to trace the exhaustion the circuit produces, to map its colonial geographies, and to ask what forms of resistances are possible in such conditions.

1.5 Structure of the Thesis

This thesis moves from the surface of the feed to the depth of its infrastructures and back.

Chapter 2: The Feed examines the feed as the surface layer of the feed-cloud circuit and the exhaustion it produces in the scrolling body. Beginning from the 4am scroll described in the opening journal entry, the chapter traces how the feed's design exhausts attention, directs the gaze, disrupts sleep, and extracts behavioral data while presenting all of this as leisure. It argues that the scrolling body performs unpaid labor for a system organized to keep that labor invisible, and that the exhaustion this produces is not individual failing but structural condition. The chapter closes with a survey of artistic, communal, and structural resistances to the feed's capture of attention, asking what interruption at the surface can and cannot reach.

Chapter 3: The Cloud descends through the threshold where behavioral data leaves the feed's surface and enters the cloud's infrastructure. It traces the exhaustion of water bodies drawn down by data centers' cooling systems, mineral bodies consumed at rates entirely disproportionate to their geological formation, and human bodies laboring invisibly across the supply chain from the mine to the data center to the content moderation center. The chapter examines the cloud's myth of immateriality as the structural counterpart to the feed's myth of free, infinite provision, and traces the colonial and racial logic through which the circuit concentrates its costs in communities and territories organized for extraction long before digital infrastructure existed. Like Chapter 2, it closes with resistances that refuse the cloud's weight and contest the terms on which it is placed.

Chapter 4: Participatory Resistance documents two participatory workshops designed and facilitated by the author as a direct response to the conditions Chapters 2 and 3 describe. Where those chapters analyze the circuit from the outside, Chapter 4 works from the inside, attempting to collectively inhabit and interrupt the exhaustion the thesis has been tracing analytically. Alternative Media Feed Design works at the feed's surface, inviting participants to collectively reimagine what a media system designed for rest and decay rather than continuous engagement might look like. Mapping the Cloud works at the cloud's depth, inviting participants to build a collective material map of their bodily and ecological entanglement within cloud infrastructure. The chapter documents each workshop's design, what happened when it was run, and what the practice produced that the theoretical analysis could not.

Chapter 5: Discussion steps back from the individual chapters to reflect on what the thesis as a whole achieved and where it reached its own limits. It returns to the images the thesis began with—the grandfather scrolling in the dark, the grandmother's funeral photographs in an unknown data center, the author staring at the phone at 4am—and asks what the research has done to the understanding of those scenes. It names the thesis's contributions honestly and its limits with equal honesty, including the positionality of a researcher writing about colonial geographies of exhaustion from a position within the Global North.

Chapter 6: Conclusion acknowledges that the circuit is still running as the reader encounters this thesis. It makes a final gesture toward what the research means beyond the document itself, addressing the reader directly as another body inside the same circuit the thesis has traced.
