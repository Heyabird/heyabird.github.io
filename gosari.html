Gosari [1]

2025

I

One of the first memories of my life is touch: holding hands with my Halmoni [2]. My small and curly Gosari hands [3] held onto her thick and bulky fingers as we walked the sunny days of Seoul. Although rough and weathered from years of labor, her hands still carried a sense of softness and warmth. Although Halmoni passed away a few years ago, I vividly remember the sensation of holding her hands. So vivid, I can still feel them on the edge of my fingers.

Another early memory rushes through, and this time it is of smell, taste, and digestion. Halmoni could pull off many dishes, but my favorite of hers was Gosari Namul [4]. I remember sitting in her kitchen, taking in the scent of perilla oil and the Gosaris she foraged with her coarse hands. My Gosari hands picked up a pair of chopsticks too big for me, reaching for the Namul. I bit into it. It was both rough and soft like my grandma. It tasted like the meat of the earth. Savory from the start, subtle sweetness seeped out as I chewed. I ate a spoonful of warm rice to balance things out. I could feel the sunlight on my back and the heat in my stomach. My belly holds this memory.

II

Halmoni moved to the states to take care of me and my brother. The three of us lived in a big white house in a rural neighborhood in Washington. In our backyard were plum trees, wild plants, and white roses.

Without knowing English, Halmoni spent her days cooking and taking walks while we went to school. To help her adjust to this new country, I taught her how to say "Beautiful" in English. Whenever she passed by a bush of roses, she shouted to me in a thick accent, "bu-ti-pool!" I corrected her. "Halmoni! It's beautiful, not bu-ti-pool!" In response, she grinned so wide her gold teeth reflected the sun.

One of the things Halmoni cooked for us was Gosari Namul. Even in America, there were Gosaris growing around us. Naturally, she carried around a plastic bag and foraged them. Even though I was happy to eat Gosari Namul, my favorite dish, I was embarrassed about her foraging. It didn't feel normal in America. And I was desperate to be normal.

Halmoni visited my high school once. She must have walked all the way from home during one of her foraging strolls. Because I was already so unpopular at school, I couldn't risk any more chances becoming more of a loser. I didn't want other kids to know that I was being raised by a grandmother, that she only spoke Korean, and that she spent her days picking up weird plants you aren't supposed to eat. It was probably illegal anyway.

So when she showed up and waved, I pretended like I didn't see her. I looked away, but I could still hear her. She muttered in Korean, "she's acting like she doesn't even know me". From now and then this mutter revisits my ears. My heart breaks each time.

III

The last time I saw Halmoni was in a cold room. We, her immediate family, were invited to say goodbye to her before we buried her body.

The funeral instructor uncovered a white box, and there she was, lying inside. She had lost most of her hair, and her body was pale like white stone. Her fingers were hardened and curled up like tree roots. But the most shocking of it all was her face. All the fat from it was gone to the point she looked like a skull. Horrified, I thought, so this is what she was all along?

Next morning, at 6 a.m., a bus arrived near the funeral home, where we stayed the night. We put our bodies and Halmoni's into the bus, which carried us for a few hours into the Korean countryside. Once we arrived, my brother, dad, and uncles carried the white box up a mountain. I followed. It was a cold day, so I could see my breath in the air.

After around thirty minutes, we arrived at a flat land. There, a hole was already dug on the ground, waiting to fit a box. I collapsed to the ground and weeped. I wondered, will I ever get over this? I still wonder.

1. Gosari (고사리) refers to both the species of bracken fern (Pteridium aquilinum) and the Korean dish where the fiddleheads of the ferns are dried and cooked.
2. Halmoni (할머니) means "grandmother" in Korean.
3. Gosari Hands (고사리 손) is a Korean metaphorical expression of a child's hands. Both children's hands and Gosaris (fiddleheads) are small and curly.
4. Namul (나물) is the general term in Korean cuisine for seasoned herbs and vegetables. We often eat Namuls as side dishes to eat with rice.
